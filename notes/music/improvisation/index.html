2. Cadences

A cadence is a chord progression that ends a phrase or section of a piece of music, we will be focusing on the following ones :

  1. Simple cadence ;
  2. Compound cadence (suspended 4 -> 3) ;
  3. Double cadence ;
  4. Long cadence.

Learning these cadences will help us harmonize the degrees 1, 5 and 4 and give us some progressions that we will be using to give rhythm to our improvisations. Practice theses cadences in all keys with different hand positions, you should be very comfortable playing them.

3. Rule of the Octave

The rule of the octave (RO) is a way to harmonize an ascending and descending scale on the bass. To harmonize a bass we shouldn’t be using the triads chords as parallel fifth (P5) and parallel octaves (P8) need to be avoided (at least in the outer voices) in classical music. Parallel voices tend to merge instead of being distinctively heard, which defies the point of having multiple voices.

The RO was built following these two steps :

  1. Determining consonant (1, 5) and dissonant (2, 3, 4, 6, 7) scale degrees ;
  2. Adding dissonances to the scales degrees that precede stable positions :
    • 5-6 dissonances to scale degrees 4 and 7 ascending ;
    • 4-3 dissonances to scale degrees 6 and 2 descending (on degree 6, the 6th is raised to create a leading tone to 5 giving the same sonority to degrees 2 and 6 and helping us modulating later on).

The 3rd degree considered partly stable / partly mobile, dissonances were added to the rising 2 and falling 4 (following the principle of dissonance before stability).

Degrees 1 and 5 are the most consonant and therefore the most stable ones (chord of the fifth as we have seen when working on cadences) whereas degrees 2, 3, 4, 6 and 7 are felt to be unstable and more mobile.

There exist some exceptions to the RO that are important to remember :

  • Scale degree 4 : takes a chord of the 5th if it does not touch 5 (coming from or going to) ;
  • Scale degree 6 : takes a chord of the 5th if it does not go to down to 5 or up to 7.

Practice all positions in all the scales until you’re very comfortable with every hand position, you should be able to play it without any hesitation and vary order of the scale degrees at will as this will be needed to play some progression schemes in the further steps.

5. Modulations

Modulations provides us a way to change keys as a mean to make music less repetitive. There are multiple ways to modulate through keys and one of them is using pivot chords (chords that are in both scale).

We will see some techniques to be able to modulate easily :

  1. From tonic to dominant using scale degree 2.
  2. From tonic to dominant using scale degree 7.

Tonic to Subdominant

These modulation will help us go back to the key we started on.

Using scale degree 4

The descending RO is a way to modulate to the dominant using scale degrees 1 - 7 - 6 - 5 from the tonic as we just seen. However, once we are on the dominant key we could use the same chords that are now the scale degrees 4 - 3 - 2 - 1 to go back to the tonic. This is possible because we flatten the scale degree 7 of the dominant to convert it to scale degree 4 of the tonic.

Relative Major and Minor keys

It is a common practice to modulate from a major / minor key to a minor / major key. To do that we will use relative keys that can be obtained as following :

  • Relative Minor from tonic to submediant | I - vi (C to Am)
  • Relative Major from tonic to mediant | i - III (Am to C)

These modulations allow to change the mood of the music played and make repeated patterns more interesting as the sound can drastically change.

Relative Minor using scale degree 7

As we previously said, we will be moving from the tonic (1st degree) to the submediant (6th degree). Going to the relative key is a fast process and a cadence may be used to confirm the new key.

Notice on the 7th degree that we are using the ascending melodic minor scale.

Relative Major I - III using scale degree 4

Now that we are on the relative minor it could be really useful to come back to the major key. To do that we will be moving from the tonic (1st degree) to the mediant (3rd degree) using the fourth degree of the scale we are modulating to.

We are now able to move through major and minor keys.

6. Improvisation

An intermediary step leaning towards improvisation is Partimenti. The objective will be to lay down a bass line following a specific structure composed of the prototypes we learned so far. An example of Partimento could be :

Once the bass line and the structure has been defined, the next step will be to improvise over this line using :

  • 2, 3 or 4 voices (4 voices are the complete chords learned in RO).
  • Rhythmic activation (duplication of a note) / displacement (offset of a note).
  • Diminutions (shorter note patterns composed of chordal and non chordal notes that usually end on a chordal note).
  • Appoggiaturas, neighbor notes, passing notes, suspended notes etc.

Texture : voices and diminutions

Defining the number of voices (2, 3 or 4) and the movement (diminutions) within them is another important part of planning an improvisation.

  • 2 voices : thinner sound but space is left to emphasize melody ;
  • 3 voices : choosing adequately the notes from the prototype to make it serve its purpose while also add movement within voices to keep an interesting melody ;
  • 4 voices : static feeling created by block chords that could compromise melody.

After the plan

Once the plan is defined, the playing part will come. Here are some key components to keep in mind :

  • Maintaining tempo : feel free to use rubato or any other technique to make your improvisation come to life but try to keep an overall constant tempo when you are playing. It will help your listeners to not lose focus and make you sound much more in control of what you are doing.

  • Exploring : do not write down every single note of your improvisation, we are not composing but rather playing music. Keep interesting patterns and structures that you may like but do not stick to the same lines every time.

  • Memorizing : memorizing patterns or melodies that you already played can be a great way to make your music more stable and give the sensation that it is going somewhere rather than playing new things every time and take the risk to lose your listener. However you should keep the right balance between repetition and variety (playing same melody after a modulation).

  • Handling mistakes : watch out for any single mistake on practice time but force yourself to continue the improvisation without stopping. Making a pause right in the middle of an improvisation is the worst thing that could happen when playing a piece.

  • Thinking ahead : be sure to not only be focused on what you are playing right now but also on what will come next (which note, chord, structure or key) to be able to transition smoothly and not loose yourself forcing you to break the tempo.

  • Expressing yourself : when improvising, you should feel what you are playing. It will guide you through the utilization of flexible tempo, velocity or sustain pedal. These are ways to make the accent over a melody to make it more interesting and personal.
