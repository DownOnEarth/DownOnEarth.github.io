Finite field Fp\mathbb F_p

For a prime pp, the set Z/pZ\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z is the set of residue classes modulo pp:

Z/pZ={a+pZ : a∈Z}={[0],[1],…,[p−1]}\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z=\{a+p\mathbb Z\ :\ a\in\mathbb Z\}=\{[0],[1],\dots,[p-1]\}

Each residue class is identified with its canonical representative in {0,1,…,p−1}\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}. Under operations (+,×)(+,\times) modulo pp, the set forms a finite field Fp\mathbb F_p such that:

Fp≅Z/pZandFp∗=Fp∖{0}\mathbb{F}_p \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\qquad and\quad \mathbb F_p^*=\mathbb F_p\setminus \{0\}

Primality is important because:

∀a∈Fp∗,∃a−1∈Fp∗ : a×a−1≡1(modp)\forall a \in \mathbb F_p^*,\quad \exists a^{-1} \in \mathbb F_p^*\ :\ a\times a^{-1}\equiv 1 \pmod p

Congruence modulo pp is defined as:

a≡b(modp)  ⟺  p∣(a−b)a \equiv b \pmod p \iff p\mid(a-b)

Note that, for 0<a<p,(−a) mod p=p−a0<a<p,\quad (-a)\bmod p=p-a.

Primitive Elements

Under repeated multiplication, every element of Fp\mathbb F_p can be used to make a subgroup HH:

∀g∈Fp∗H=⟨g⟩={g,g2,g3,…}\forall g \in \mathbb F_p^*\qquad H=\langle g\rangle=\{g,g^2,g^3,\ldots\}

A primitive element of Fp\mathbb F_p is an element whose subgroup H=Fp∗H=\mathbb F_p^* and is therefore called a generator of the multiplicative group (Fp∗)(\mathbb F_p^*):

∀a∈Fp∗,∃n∈N: a≡gn(modp)\forall a\in \mathbb F_p^*,\quad \exists n\in \mathbb N :\ a\equiv g^n\pmod p

The element g∈Fp∗g\in\mathbb F_p^* is a generator if and only if for every distinct prime factor qq of p−1p-1:

g(p−1)/q≢1(modp)g^{(p-1)/q}\not\equiv 1\pmod p

Euclidean Algorithm

For a,b∈Z>0a,b\in \mathbb Z_{>0}, by Euclidean division:

a=qb+r,q∈Z≥0,0≤r<ba=qb+r, \qquad q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}, \qquad 0\le r < b

Then (a,b)←(b,r)(a,b)\leftarrow (b,r) until b=0b=0, the final aa is the gcd\text{gcd}.

This works because any number that divides both aa and bb also divides r=a−qbr=a-qb and vice versa. Therefore replacing the larger number with the remainder does not change the greatest common divisor.

gcd(a,b)=gcd(b,r)=gcd(b,a mod b)\text{gcd}(a,b)=\text{gcd}(b,r)=\text{gcd}(b,a \bmod b)

Extended Euclidean Algorithm

ax+by=gcd(a,b)ax+by=\text{gcd}(a,b)

Fermat’s Little Theorem

For a prime pp and any integer aa, the integer ap−aa^p-a is multiple of pp:

∀p∈P,∀a∈Z,ap≡a(modp)\forall p\in \mathbb P,\forall a\in \mathbb Z,\qquad a^p\equiv a\pmod p

Equivalently if pp and aa are co-prime:

∀p∈P,∀a∈Z,p∤a  ⟹  ap−1≡1(modp)\forall p\in \mathbb P,\forall a\in \mathbb Z,\quad p\nmid a \implies a^{p-1}\equiv 1\pmod p

Euler’s Totient and Theorem

Euler’s Theorem states that if gcd(a,n)=1\text{gcd}(a,n)=1, then:

aφ(n)≡1(modn)a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv 1\pmod n

With φ(n)\varphi(n) the Euler’s Totient function, that counts the positive integers from 11 to nn that are coprime1 to nn. For an integer nn, whose distinct prime factors are p1,…,pkp_1, \ldots, p_k:

φ(n)=n∏i=1k(1−1pi)\varphi(n)=n\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_i}\right)

For a prime number pp:

φ(p)=p−1\varphi(p)=p-1

a∈Fp is a quadratic residue  ⟺  ∃x∈Fp : x2≡a(modp)a \in \mathbb F_p \text{ is a quadratic residue} \iff \exists x \in \mathbb F_p\ :\ x^2 \equiv a \pmod p

For a prime pp, let Qp,Qˉp⊆FpQ_p, \bar{Q}_p \subseteq \mathbb F_p denote the sets of nonzero quadratic residues and quadratic nonresidues:

Qp⋅Qp⊆QpQp⋅Qˉp⊆QˉpQˉp⋅Qˉp⊆Qp \begin{aligned} Q_p \cdot Q_p &\subseteq Q_p\\ Q_p \cdot \bar{Q}_p &\subseteq \bar{Q}_p\\ \bar{Q}_p \cdot \bar{Q}_p &\subseteq Q_p \end{aligned}

Euler’s Criterion and Legendre Symbol

By Euler’s criterion, if aa is a nonzero quadratic residue modulo pp, then:

a(p−1)/2≡1(modp),p>2 a^{(p-1)/2}\equiv1\pmod p,\qquad p>2

Where the Legendre symbol is:

a(p−1)/2≡(ap)(modp)={0if a≡0(modp)1if a is a nonzero quadratic residue mod p−1if a is a quadratic nonresidue mod p a^{(p-1)/2}\equiv \left(\frac{a}{p}\right)\pmod p= \begin{cases} \begin{aligned} 0 & \quad\text{if }a\equiv 0\pmod p \\ 1 & \quad\text{if }a\text{ is a nonzero quadratic residue}\bmod p \\ -1 & \quad\text{if }a\text{ is a quadratic nonresidue}\bmod p \end{aligned} \end{cases}

Chinese Remainder Theorem

The Chinese Remainder Theorem states that a system of simultaneous congruences has a unique solution modulo N=∏i=1kniN=\prod_{i=1}^{k} n_i provided that the moduli nin_i are pairwise coprime2.

{x≡a1(modn1)x≡a2(modn2)⋮x≡ak(modnk) \begin{cases} x \equiv a_1 \pmod{n_1}\\ x \equiv a_2 \pmod{n_2}\\ \vdots\\ x \equiv a_k \pmod{n_k} \end{cases}
x=∑i=1kaiNi(Ni−1 mod ni)(modN),Ni=Nnix=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_i N_i (N_i^{-1} \bmod n_i) \pmod N,\qquad N_i=\frac{N}{n_i}

Operations

Addition

For a,b∈Z/pZa,b\in \mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z the addition c≡a+b(modp)c\equiv a+b\pmod p is computed as:

x=p−a,c={a+b,b<xb−x,b≥xx=p-a,\quad c=\begin{cases}a+b,\quad b<x\\ b-x,\quad b\ge x\end{cases}

This is done as a practical way to avoid computing large numbers such as (a+b)(a+b) overflowing what an integer can encode.

Subtraction

Subtraction is achieved by using the additive inverse:

a−b=a+(−b),b+(−b)≡e(+)≡0(modp)a-b=a+(-b),\quad b+(-b)\equiv e^{(+)}\equiv0\pmod p

Multiplication (double and add)

Multiplication can be viewed as repeated addition:

a⋅b=a+a+⋅⋅⋅+a⏟b timesa\cdot b=\underbrace{a+a+\cdot\cdot\cdot+a}_{b \text{ times}}

However, for b≥1b\ge1, repeated addition requires b−1b-1 additions. As every nonnegative integer can be written as the sum of powers of 22, if we take the binary form of the multiplier bb, it is possible to reduce the computation to:

a⋅b≡∑i=0kbi2ia(modp),bi∈{0,1}a \cdot b\equiv \sum_{i=0}^{k}b_i 2^i a \pmod p,\quad b_i \in \{0,1\}

where kk is the index of the most significant bit of bb, this reduces the number of modular additions from O(b)\mathcal{O}(b) to O(log⁡b)\mathcal{O}(\log b).

Division

Division is achieved by using the multiplicative inverse

ab=a×b−1,b≠0,b×b−1≡e(×)≡1(modp).\frac ab = a\times b^{-1},\qquad b\ne0,\qquad b\times b^{-1}\equiv e^{(\times)}\equiv1\pmod p.

Exponentiation (square and multiply)

Exponentiation can be viewed as repeated multiplication:

ab=a×a×⋅⋅⋅×a⏟b timesa^b=\underbrace{a\times a\times\cdot\cdot\cdot\times a}_{b \text{ times}}

However, for b≥1b\ge1, repeated multiplication requires b−1b-1 multiplications. As every nonnegative integer can be written as the sum of powers of 22, if we take the binary form of the exponent bb, it is possible to reduce the computation to:

ab≡∏i=0kabi2i(modp),bi∈{0,1}, b∈Z≥0a^b\equiv\prod_{i=0}^{k}a^{b_i 2^i} \pmod p,\qquad b_i \in \{0,1\},\ b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}

where kk is the index of the most significant bit of bb, this reduces the number of modular multiplications from O(b)\mathcal{O}(b) to O(log⁡b)\mathcal{O}(\log b).
